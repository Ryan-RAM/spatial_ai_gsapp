Misty Forest Chase is a self-running 3D simulation built for the Distance + Proximity assignment. A thief has to steal a diamond and escape through an exit; a police officer guards it and gives chase. Nobody plays: both are AIs, and each one knows the world only through distances. Each of four distances drives one sense: sight, hearing, walking and the sense of place.

Sight uses the straight 3D distance between the eyes. You see someone only if they are within 9 cells, inside your 120 degree forward cone, and nothing solid is on the line: a tree, a bush, a rock or a platform floor. So the officer here sees the first thief, but not the one behind the tree, the one behind its back, or the one lost in the fog. Hearing uses the same straight line, but a wall does not stop sound, it muffles it. Every cell of material the line crosses adds a cost: a bush adds 1.5, a rock adds 6. Ten cells of open air are heard; seven cells through a bush and a rock count as 14.5, beyond the 12-cell hearing range.

Walking uses the path distance, the bonus metric: the shortest walk around obstacles, computed with Dijkstra’s algorithm. Here the straight line crosses a wall of bushes and is 6 cells; the walk around its end is 11.7. Both AIs move along these paths. The sense of place uses the perceptual distance. From every cell we cast 362 rays in 3D and record how far each one gets before hitting something, up to 9 cells. That list is the cell’s visibility fingerprint, drawn here as a barcode. These are real cells from the default map: A and B are 15.5 cells apart but their barcodes are almost the same, a difference of 22. A nook looks very different: 78. For comparison, a typical pair of cells differs by about 69.

Checking the axioms on every map: Euclidean and path distance are true metrics. The acoustic distance is only a semimetric: the triangle inequality fails in 598 of 4,000 random tests, because a straight line through a rock sounds farther than a detour around it. The perceptual distance is a pseudometric: two different places can have identical fingerprints, which is exactly what getting lost means. The game itself is balanced: over 320 simulated rounds on 8 maps the police wins 49.7 percent. Half of the police wins come before the theft; the tracker in the diamond accounts for the 10 percent caught carrying it. One round in 320 ran out of time.
