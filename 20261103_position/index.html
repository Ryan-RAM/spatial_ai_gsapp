Misty Forest Chase: Position + Movement

A diamond lies somewhere in a foggy forest with a canopy walkway three cells up. A thief must steal it and get out through one of two exits, on the west and east sides; a police officer patrols around the diamond, chases the thief on sight and has to catch it. The diamond carries a tracker: once it is taken, the police always knows where it is. There are no dead ends: every path loops back somewhere. Nobody plays: both are simple AIs, and each step they act once. The game is a way to compare four distances, because each one drives a different sense.

This week the forest also reads where things are relative to each other and how they move: above or below, behind, adjacent, between and among; toward or away, up or down a ladder, around the diamond and past the officer. Each relation lights up on the floor plans, in the 3D view and in the panel. Some of them change what happens: owls perched on the canopy hoot when the thief comes next to them, and an officer standing between the thief and an exit or the diamond changes the thief’s plan.

    Event log

      How position and movement are read

      Prepositions of place and movement, turned into rules on the grid. Every rule is checked after each step (src/sim/relations.js); the thresholds are in config.relations and config.owls. In the diagrams, blue is the police, orange the thief, purple an owl and yellow the relation itself.

      Position and movement

      Position

      Above / below

      Rule. One agent is on the canopy, the other on the ground, at most 2 columns apart on the plan.

      Shown as. Double rings at both columns on both floor plans, with ▲ ABOVE / ▼ BELOW tags; a vertical line in 3D.

      Position

      Behind

      Rule. Within 9 cells (the fog), and more than 90° from where the other faces. The cone is 120° wide, so behind is never seen.

      Shown as. The rear half-disc of the observer is shaded and pulses while someone stands in it; a BEHIND tag on that agent.

      Position

      Adjacent

      Rule. No occupiable cell in between: one walking step (8 directions, no cutting a blocked corner, ladders count), or the same column on the other level.

      Shown as. One yellow outline wrapped around both cells. Checked for the thief and the police, the diamond, each exit and each owl.

      Position

      Between

      Rule. On the plan, the officer projects inside the segment from the thief to the target and lies within 1.6 cells of it. Target: the diamond before the theft, each exit after it.

      Shown as. A dashed line from the thief to the target, crossed out at the officer, with an IN THE WAY or CUT OFF tag.

      Position

      Among

      Rule. At least 5 of the 8 cells around are solid on that level: trees, bushes or rocks on the ground, leaf clumps on the canopy. The most common one names it.

      Shown as. A dotted 3 × 3 frame in the agent’s colour around the cover.

      Movement

      Toward / away from

      Rule. After each step, the mover’s path distance to where the other one stood is compared with before the step.

      Shown as. An arrow from the old cell to the new one: solid when closing in, dashed when opening up.

      Movement

      Up / down

      Rule. The agent’s level changed in this step, so it used a ladder.

      Shown as. An ↑ UP or ↓ DOWN tag rises or sinks from the ladder and fades; an arrow cone in 3D; a line in the log.

      Movement

      Around

      Rule. While an agent stays within 8 cells of the diamond, the turning of its bearing from the diamond is added up. At 270° it went around.

      Shown as. The trail is drawn as a thick line with an arrowhead and an AROUND tag at the diamond, then fades.

      Movement

      Past

      Rule. The path distance between them falls, bottoms out at 4 cells or less and grows again, while the officer never saw the thief.

      Shown as. A ring at the closest point with a » PAST tag, then it fades.

      Behaviours triggered by position

      Behaviour · intrinsic capability

      Owls warn

      Rule. Three owls perch on the canopy. When the thief is adjacent to one, the owl hoots (then rests for 30 steps). The officer hears it if the acoustic distance is 16 or less, and goes to the owl. The thief knows: it never picks a waiting spot next to an owl.

      Shown as. Owl icons on the plans and in 3D; their eyes light up and a dashed frame appears while the thief is next to them; purple rings for the hoot.

      Behaviour · placement

      Cut-off

      Rule. While escaping, an exit with the officer between it and the thief (where the thief last noticed the officer) counts as 30 cells farther. The thief turns for the other exit.

      Shown as. CUT OFF tag at the officer and a log line when the thief changes exit.

      Behaviour · placement

      Guarded

      Rule. While the waiting thief can see the officer standing between it and the diamond, it does not go for it, and keeps waiting.

      Shown as. IN THE WAY tag at the officer; the thief’s mode reads "Waiting: the police is in the way".

      How the four senses work

      See: Euclidean distance

      dE(a, b) = √((xa − xb)² + (ya − yb)² + (za − zb)²)

      Measured between eye points, 1.5 cells above the floor; the canopy floor is 3 cells above the ground. An agent sees the other when all three hold:

      • dE ≤ 9: farther than that, the fog hides everything.
      • The other is inside the forward cone: 120° wide and 60° up and down from the direction the agent faces. Vision only looks forward; whatever is behind or beside the cone is not seen.
      • The 3D segment between the eyes does not pass through a solid volume: tree, bush, rock, leaf clump or platform floor. Open air in the canopy blocks nothing, so you can look down from a platform edge, or up through a gap.

      Because each agent has its own facing, the thief can see the police while the police does not see the thief, or the other way round.

      Hear: acoustic distance

      dA(a, b) = dE(a, b) + Σk mk

      Sound travels in a straight line, like light, but solids do not stop it: they muffle it. For every grid cell of solid material the straight line passes through, a muffling cost mk is added. The cost is in cells: one cell of rock makes a footstep as faint as 6 more cells of open air.

      Material crossedMuffling cost mk
      Leaf clump+1
      Bush+1.5
      Tree+3
      Rock+6
      Platform floor+6
      Open air+0

      Example: 5 cells apart with one bush and one rock in between gives dA = 5 + 1.5 + 6 = 12.5, just too far to hear.

      • Whenever someone moves, the other hears it if dA ≤ 12, and knows where the sound came from.
      • Hearing has no direction: front, back, left, right, up and down all count. Through open air, dA = dE, so you can hear someone on the ground from a platform edge.

      Walk: path distance

      dP(a, b) = min over walks a → b of Σ step costs

      Step costs: 1 straight, √2 diagonal, 3 for a ladder. A diagonal step may not cut the corner of an obstacle. Computed with Dijkstra's algorithm on the walkable cells of both levels.

      • It is how far an agent really has to go: both AIs move along these shortest paths, and the thief only flees to cells it reaches before the police.
      • Two agents on either side of a hedge, or one right above the other, can be close in dE but far in dP.

      Sense of place: perceptual distance

      dV(a, b) = √(Σi (ri(a) − ri(b))²)

      From the eye point of every walkable cell, 362 rays are cast in 3D: 72 directions (every 5°) on five elevation rings (−60°, −30°, 0°, +30°, +60°), plus straight up and straight down. Each ray stops at the first solid volume or at 9 cells.

      • The list of the 362 ray lengths ri is the cell's visibility fingerprint. It covers all 360° and does not depend on facing: it describes the space around a position.
      • Because the fog cuts every ray at 9, open places have very similar fingerprints, while every nook in the thicket is different.
      • The same rays draw the faint all-around view in the 3D scene; the bright cone is the subset of rays the agent is looking along right now.

      The two AIs

      Police

      • Patrolling: guards the diamond. Walks to open spots, usually within 10 cells of the diamond and sometimes anywhere on the map, glancing to the side every other step.
      • Investigating a sound: on hearing the thief, turns toward the sound and takes the shortest path to where it came from.
      • Chasing the thief: on seeing the thief, runs to where it saw it. Within 5 cells of the thief it sprints at 1.2 times the normal speed. It catches the thief on the same or a neighbouring cell.
      • Scanning around: at a sound, or where it lost sight of the thief, turns a full circle, then goes back to patrolling.
      • Tracking the diamond: the diamond has a tracker. From the moment the thief picks it up, the officer knows where it is at every step and runs straight for it along the shortest path, sprinting at 1.2 times the normal speed for the first 15 cells, then tiring back to normal speed (still sprinting whenever within 5 cells). The thief does not get this: it still only knows what it sees and hears.

      Thief

      • Sneaking: heads for a hidden spot within 8 cells of the diamond, one cell every two steps. Sneaking steps are soft: they are heard only within an acoustic distance of 7 instead of 12.
      • Waiting for a chance / Looking around: near the diamond, waits until it has not heard the police for 8 steps, turning the head now and then.
      • Going for the diamond: runs to it, one cell per step. Taking it is instant.
      • Escaping with the diamond: runs for the exit with the biggest head start over where it last noticed the police. The diamond is heavy: the thief runs at 0.8 times the normal speed.
      • Holding breath: hears the police, but it is not close: freezes, makes no sound and turns toward the noise.
      • Fleeing / Hiding: when seen, about to be seen, or hearing the police come closer, runs to a cell it reaches first, out of the police's sight, then lies low before trying again.

      The thief wins by leaving through an exit with the diamond. The police wins by catching the thief, or if the diamond is still in the forest after 500 steps. A new round starts about 2 seconds after the end of one.

      Are these distances metrics?

      Checked on the current map: every walkable cell is a point. Symmetry and the triangle inequality are tested on random triples; "identity of indiscernibles" asks whether d = 0 only when the two positions are the same cell.

      DistanceNon-negativity
      d(a, b) ≥ 0      		Symmetry
      d(a, b) = d(b, a)      		Triangle inequality
      d(a, c) ≤ d(a, b) + d(b, c)      		Identity of indiscernibles
      d(a, b) = 0 ⇔ a = b      		Verdict

      How this answers the assignment

      1. At least two metrics, computed between two positions and between two agents. Four distances are used: Euclidean, acoustic, path and perceptual. The distance panel shows all four between the two agents at every step; the metric check computes them between thousands of pairs of positions, and shows which ones really are metrics.
      2. Visualize and compare them graphically. In 3D, the solid line is the Euclidean distance, the dots on it mark where the sound is muffled (acoustic distance), the dashed line is the shortest path, and the rays show the fingerprint. The chart plots all four over the round against the vision and hearing limits.
      3. Far in one metric, near in another. The automatic hint describes these moments as they happen: stacked on two levels, a hedge in between, near but muffled, back to back, in view but a long walk away, or far apart in places that look alike. The game itself depends on them: the thief can be within earshot but out of sight, or the other way round.
      4. Bonus: distance around obstacles. The path distance is a shortest walk around solid cells and over ladders between two levels (Dijkstra, 8 directions). Both AIs move along it, and the thief uses it to find escape cells it reaches first.
